Roblox · 3D retail experience

Melville Clothing

A 3D interactive clothing store inspired by Brandy Melville, a clothing retailer. I designed and developed it in Roblox Studio, including the environment, displays, fitting rooms, lighting, and shopping features.

Why I made it

I work at Brandy Melville's Montréal location, and I thought it would be cool to recreate it in a video game. It's a place that's a big part of my life and that I know really well, so I wanted to bring that familiar environment into Roblox.

Working there, I've seen Brandy Melville's cult following firsthand. I knew there was an audience who would recognize the store's iconic style and want to explore and shop in a digital version. My goal was to recreate the atmosphere people recognize and love.

Building the store

I built the store in Roblox Studio, using the building tools provided in the software and Lua (Luau) for scripting. I used those tools to recreate the building and store layout, with custom objects for the details of the real store.

To get the closest resemblance, I combined normal mapping for texture depth with realistic lighting. For more complex objects, I used Blender and an AI mesh generator, working from photos to recreate items you would find in the actual store.

Game systems

Making it work as a store

I built the experience around browsing and trying on clothing. The Luau logic identifies the selected clothing asset and applies it to the player's avatar. Players can toggle or remove pieces as they try different outfits, then buy the clothing they want at checkout.

Because trying on outfits is central to the game, I placed mirrors throughout the store so players can check their look while browsing. The fitting rooms and scripted doors are part of making the space work as a store, too.

Optimization

As I added more clothing, manually handling and loading each item became harder to manage. With so many assets, the store loaded slowly and textures appeared blurry while loading. The racks, shelves, and displays were filling up, and the store still needed to be usable as that collection grew.

I used an ACC Loader to help load the clothing and make the growing collection easier to manage as I continued adding items to the store.

Challenges

Changing clothing costs

On July 7, Roblox announced plans to raise the combined cost of uploading and publishing a 2D clothing item from 20 to 800 Robux, a 3,900% increase. Six days later, it revised that to 90 Robux, still 350% above the previous cost.

For a store with a growing clothing collection, those per-item costs made adding new clothing a bigger upfront investment, forcing me to rethink my approach to the game.

Roblox's fee announcement ↗

Reaching younger players

Players under 16 were part of my intended audience. Roblox's new age-based publishing rules introduced an evaluation process before an experience could reach them, making it harder to reach part of the audience I had built the store for.

Roblox's audience requirements ↗

Launch

Finishing the build was only part of launching it. I moved the experience into the Melville group, worked through Roblox's publishing and visibility requirements, and set up clothing sales around the store.

I promoted it on TikTok, sharing videos of the store so people could see the experience before visiting. After 90+ hours of work, the project has reached 1.5K+ visits and sold 850+ clothing items.

What I learned

Melville started as an environment-design project and became an exercise in building a complete product: designing around user behavior, managing a growing content library, working within platform constraints, optimizing an asset-heavy environment, and shipping to real users.
